# Related work and attribution

This project asks how many separate observers can accurately predict the **current outcome of a projective measurement of a driven qubit**, when each observer receives only a fraction of its dephasing output. The total source dynamics are fixed. Each observer may process its own past output but cannot act back on the source or communicate with the other observers.

The central comparison concerns two precisely specified receiver classes. The [quantum limit](../science/quantum_limit.md) permits arbitrary quantum memory and a terminal collective measurement on the received past field. The [diffusive homodyne limit](../science/homodyne.md) permits arbitrary predictable choice of the measured quadrature phase and arbitrary causal classical processing. Both concern the exact noncommuting source, rather than assuming that its state is an underlying classical telegraph process.

Nulling, adaptive photodetection, finite-state quantum tracking, nonlinear filtering, and multiple-observer conditional dynamics all have substantial precedents. The contribution developed here is the quantitative connection between these ingredients: a matching quantum receiver converse for partial output, a uniform comparison for every allowed diffusive homodyne phase policy, and their implications for observer number at a prescribed error. The sections below identify the closest precedents and the boundaries of that connection. Full bibliographic records, including available author preprints, are in [references.bib](references.bib).

## Classical filtering

[Wonham (1964)](https://doi.org/10.1137/0302028) derived the finite-state nonlinear filtering equations for a Markov signal observed in noise. The binary Wonham filter used here is an established instance of that theory. [Van Handel's thesis (2007), §1.2](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~rvan/thesisf070108.pdf), provides an explicit two-state example. The publication year of the original article is 1964, as recorded by SIAM and [Wonham's publication list](https://www.control.toronto.edu/~wonham/Publications.html).

The stationary binary comparator and its small-error logarithm belong to this classical filtering problem. They should not be attributed to a new quantum receiver mechanism. In [the homodyne analysis](../science/homodyne.md), the project-specific step is a relative comparison with the exact quantum diffusion. A convex solution of the comparator's Poisson equation has a gradient bound on the scale of the comparator's own error. This controls the approximation even when that error tends to zero, and applies uniformly to arbitrary predictable quadrature phases. Merely replacing the driven source by a telegraph process would not establish this statement.

The modified-Bessel identities used to normalize the comparator's invariant distribution and expand its error are standard. The relevant power series and integral representations are in the [NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions, §10.31](https://dlmf.nist.gov/10.31) and [§10.32](https://dlmf.nist.gov/10.32).

## Continuous quantum measurement

Quantum filtering provides the measurement model and conditional-state equations. [Bouten, van Handel, and James (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1137/060651239) develop quantum conditional expectation, nondemolition observations, quantum stochastic calculus, and the reference-probability and innovations derivations of the filters. In their [author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0601741), §§5.2–5.3 specify output measurements and the filtering problem, §6.1 treats homodyne detection, §6.2 treats photon counting, and §7 treats innovations. These foundations justify estimating a present system observable using compatible earlier output measurements.

The interpretation of distinct measurements on the same outgoing field as different quantum trajectories is also established; see [Wiseman and Milburn (1993)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.47.1652). Broader treatments are [Wiseman and Milburn, *Quantum Measurement and Control* (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511813948), especially Chapters 3–4, and [Jacobs, *Quantum Measurement Theory and its Applications* (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139179027), especially Chapters 1 and 3. These sources supply the language of output monitoring, measurement backaction, imperfect detection, and conditional states.

**Terminology matters for the receiver comparison.** [Wiseman and Toombes (1999), §V.B](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9903023), use the heading “Adaptive Homodyne Detection” for a finite-local-oscillator displaced-photon-counting scheme. Its oscillator amplitude changes after a click. The present homodyne lower bound instead concerns a **diffusive quadrature-current observation**, with an adaptively chosen phase. Finite-local-oscillator counting is outside that class. The theorem also does not cover every possible Gaussian preprocessing operation simply because its name contains “homodyne.”

Receiver-dependent knowledge of an open system predates this project. [Gambetta and Wiseman (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.64.042105) compare several monitoring schemes for a radiatively damped atom with an unknown dynamical parameter. Their measures of parameter knowledge and conditional-state purity differ from the present error for guessing a current projective outcome under a fixed output budget.

## Nulling and finite-state tracking

Kennedy's binary coherent-state receiver displaces one hypothesis to vacuum and uses photon detection to distinguish it from the other. The original citation is R. S. Kennedy, *A Near-Optimum Receiver for the Binary Coherent State Quantum Channel*, MIT Research Laboratory of Electronics, Quarterly Progress Report 108, pp. 219–225 (1973). Dolinar subsequently derived an optimum adaptive receiver for the binary coherent-state channel; his [original report](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/56414/RLE_QPR_111_VII.pdf) also records the Kennedy reference. The dark-reference construction and the information carried by a click are inherited receiver principles.

The receiver used here keeps one binary guess, displaces the output so that the guessed pointer state is dark, and flips the guess upon a click. Its exact stationary error follows from a closed pair of source–guess moments. This construction is related to adaptive nulling; it is not a claim to have invented nulling or to have rederived the finite-pulse Dolinar optimum.

[Wiseman and Toombes (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.60.2474) already exhibit adaptive two-state jumping for resonance fluorescence. [Karasik and Wiseman (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.106.020406) establish that a suitably monitored ergodic qubit can be tracked with a two-state classical apparatus, and their [stability analysis](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.84.052120) examines the resulting finite-state tracking schemes. Their setting concerns physically realizable pure-state ensembles under sufficiently complete bath monitoring. These results are direct precedents for small classical controllers and adaptive ensemble tracking. They do not supply the optimum current-outcome error for a partially observed driven dephasing source, or a converse allowing the partial observer to retain all received modes coherently.

Binary quantum minimum-error discrimination itself is also imported. The finite discrimination experiment inside the converse uses the standard Helstrom expression; see [Helstrom (1969)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01007479) and [Helstrom's monograph (1976)](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/quantum-detection-and-estimation-theory/helstrom/978-0-12-340050-5). The project-specific argument is the physical instrument on the inaccessible output, the way a genuine rank-one event screens all older receiver memory, and the interval-overlap and renewal estimates that turn that finite discrimination bound into an exact-source tracking bound.

## Several observers and objectivity

[Dziarmaga, Dalvit, and Zurek (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.69.022109) are a direct conceptual predecessor. They formulate conditional dynamics for several observers monitoring different portions of an environment, introduce a superobserver description, and study agreement through relative purity. Their examples include photodetection and homodyne monitoring of resonance fluorescence, as well as pointer-state behavior in a harmonic-oscillator setting. Agreement between separate environmental observers and its dependence on the monitoring scheme are therefore established questions. The present result adds an optimized current-outcome error and its precision-dependent observer count for a fixed driven-dephasing source, including a bound against arbitrary partial-output quantum memory.

[García-Pintos and del Campo (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3390/e23111527) bound differences between state assignments made with unequal monitoring efficiencies, using quantities such as conditional purity and entropy. Their comparison makes finite environmental access operationally significant. Its target is disagreement between assigned states; it does not optimize a receiver's error for a specified terminal binary outcome.

[Kiely and colleagues (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1103/hn78-7xx3) connect classical objectivity to the precision with which fragments reveal an initially encoded parameter. Their spin-star model has a static quantum-nondemolition interaction, and they compare quantum Fisher information with information obtained from specified fragment observables. This establishes that measurement choice can strongly affect precision-based redundancy. Here the source is continually driven, its Hamiltonian does not commute with the recorded observable, and the task is to predict the present outcome from the past output. The receiver optimization and the stationary error criterion are correspondingly different.

The observer number in this project counts observers whose **individual marginal error** is below a threshold. The result also controls pairwise disagreement through that common target. It does not assert that every observer is simultaneously correct with a fixed probability as the number of observers grows.

## Waveform and change-point detection

[Tsang and Nair (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.86.042115) derive quantum limits for waveform detection. Their fidelity bounds and Kennedy-type receivers show how nulling a pure reference can give zero false alarms and favorable detection exponents, including stochastic alternatives. Their §§V–VI are especially relevant to the optical mechanism here. This work already rules out attributing the qualitative benefit of a dark reference, or its advantage over homodyne detection, to the present project. Its hypotheses concern detection over a specified observation interval; it does not give the stationary present-state Bayes optimum of the partially observed driven source.

[Tsang (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.108.170502) develops continuous quantum hypothesis testing using stochastic likelihood equations for diffusive and counting observations, including hybrid classical–quantum dynamics. This provides a general framework for inference between dynamical hypotheses. The present work fixes a single source law and optimizes how its outgoing field reveals a current projective outcome.

[Sentís, Martínez-Vargas, and Muñoz-Tapia (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.98.052305) study online identification of a single quantum change point in a finite sequence. Their unambiguous strategy may return an inconclusive answer, and in specified overlap regimes local sequential strategies attain collective-measurement performance. This is an important precedent for simple online receivers. Exact identification of one change location with an inconclusive outcome differs from a mandatory binary answer about the current state of a continually changing source.

[Fanizza, Hirche, and Calsamiglia (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.131.020602) derive quantum quickest-change-detection limits. For the relevant support conditions, the leading detection delay is governed by a relative entropy and the false-alarm constraint. They explicitly discuss the exceptional case of nonnested supports, including pure states, where finite delay with zero false alarms is possible. Consequently, removal of a false-alarm logarithm by a quantum receiver is already a known phenomenon. The present converse must establish more than that observation: it applies to repeated current-outcome tracking in the exact noncommuting source and matches a causal receiver's leading Bayes error.

## Contribution and limits of the comparison

The central claim is the conjunction of three quantitative statements, with their full assumptions and proofs in the scientific account:

| Statement | Role beyond the inherited ingredient |
|---|---|
| A simple displaced-counting receiver has an exact stationary error. | Gives an attainable benchmark for the driven Hermitian-dephasing source at every positive drive and received rate. |
| The optimum partial-output quantum error has the same leading coefficient. | Controls arbitrary causal quantum memory by a physical lost-output instrument, exact field overlaps, and a uniform renewal estimate. |
| The optimum diffusive homodyne error retains a leading logarithmic penalty in the weak-fragment, small-error regime, even with arbitrary adaptive phase. | Connects the classical comparator to the exact quantum source through a relative, policy-uniform bound, with a fixed real quadrature attaining the leading error. |

The resulting observer-count separation follows from these matched receiver limits at fixed total dephasing. The quantum theorem is uniform for received fractions at most one half. The homodyne asymptotic additionally takes the received fraction to zero. Complete output access is a distinct endpoint: retaining the entire output coherently can outperform the nulling receiver, as explained in [the endpoint analysis](../science/quantum_limit.md#10-complete-output-access-is-a-genuine-exception).

These comparisons delimit the contribution relative to the works cited here. They are not a claim that the receiver components, classical logarithm, environmental-observer question, or general benefit of quantum change detection are new. Nor do they establish priority over every possible formulation of a tracking problem. The precise exact-source theorems, receiver classes, and asymptotic domains are the basis for assessing the contribution.
